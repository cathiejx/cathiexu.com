The stimulus is Haruki Murakami’s essay “Even If I Had a Long Ponytail Back Then.” At first glance, it’s clear that my writing is far too long to be just *one*

In “Even If I Had a Long Ponytail Back Then,” Haruki Murakami uses **scene and description** in order to **show the contrast between his own and others’ paces
in life.**

*In the rubric, this is 1 point. It names a choice (uses …), and an impact (in order to …). In reality, from just this, the rest of the paragraph is difficult
to do well. The topic sentence promises two things (scene **and** description), setting you up to prove far too much.*

His main message in this essay is that everyone will live differently, and you should be comfortable with your own journey despite. He first illustrates this
idea of individual lives with his **metaphorical description** of his running shoes. He states that the Mizunos are more fit for him because “they’re light, and
the cushioning of the sole is a little hard…this brand of shoes doesn’t have any extra bells and whistles. This is just my personal preference, nothing more.
Each person has his own likes.” (Page 2)

*“Metaphorical description” doesn’t seem like the correct way to word this here. Something like “... by using his running shoes as a metaphor. He describes that
the Mizunos are more fit for him …” would work better.*

With his emphasis on the fact that it’s his own taste, he shows that each person will have different needs, **which he later also emphasizes with the quote
“Still, runners** sense small details that set one shoe off from another, and are always looking for this psychological edge.” (Page 2)

*He is trying to use the running shoes to show how each person has different needs! But what about how he writes into the metaphor? You’re leaving me hanging,
why? The quote is right there for you to write about. Instead, you’ve gotten distracted by another quote.*

*In general, if you have something you want to do, you shouldn’t get distracted by more quotes, more evidence. You can worry about that in revision. I often end
up forgetting what matters to me most, because I’m so focused on padding myself with so many other little things. But it’s truly better to take one sharp shot
than it is to take thousands of little ones. Remember what you’re trying to say. Less is more.*

With this metaphor, he is implicitly telling the reader that people’s lives are like running shoes — different people will want and need **different “small
details.” He continues** …

*You’re also not writing about description or scene anymore, which is what you promised in your topic sentence. You’re writing about a metaphor, which makes me
think that you wanted to write about metaphors and not the scene and description in the first place. So why not promise this in your topic sentence?*

*In general, you should only promise things that you will do. Do you look like a saint volunteering to do everything? Probably. Maybe. Okay, fine, yes. But
trust me, it’s far more annoying when you take the opportunity from someone else by saying you’ll do it, and then end up not. You look worse than if you had
just done nothing.*

*Also, why are we moving on again already?? This is three quotes, far more than you need in one paragraph! You should have two, and fully pull apart those two.*

… He continues to to represent the ways runners differ by describing others around him. He puts the most emphasis on the running girls from Harvard, describing
them with detail. He says that they are “so bright, so healthy, attractive, and serious, brimming with self-confidence.” (Page 2) **He then juxtaposes them with
himself**, saying that “compared to them [he’s] pretty used to losing...but these girls probably don’t know as much as [he does] about pain. And, quite
naturally, there might not be a need for them to know it.” (Page 2)

*Now you’re talking about juxtaposition! You’ve barely written about description, and you haven’t written about scene at all, and now you’re writing about
juxtaposition! Are you trying to give me an aneurysm? That’s the wrong body part to throw in circles, if you want money!*

He uses this quote to show that although he was not as **“energetic”** as they were, he learned the life lessons that he as an individual needed. Additionally,
by saying that there was **“not a need”** for them to know the pain, he emphasizes the fact that other people have separate things to take away from life.

*But you promised description in the topic sentence? There is literally description right there in the quote for you to use … instead, you’re just summarizing
everything that he’s trying to say. This reads more like a literature analysis, where you’re trying to write about the theme of the piece.*

*In general, summary is never good analysis. There’s a reason the rubric asks you to focus on specific words and details, and that’s to prevent you from
summarizing. (Which is kind of ironic, I can’t lie. You were focusing on specific words and details.) The things you say in a conversation should add something
more, from yourself.*

To build onto this, he further shows that he is an individual by saying that **“even if [he] had a long ponytail back then, [he doubts] if it would have swung
so proudly as these girls’ ponytails do.” (Page 3)**

*That’s four quotes. Are you tired yet? Might as well add another 1430, while you’re at it.*

Here, he uses the **symbolism** of the long ponytail to make an **abstraction** on his own life.

*Symbolism? Symbolism?? Abstraction? Abstraction?? First description, then a metaphor, juxtaposition, and now symbolism, and now abstraction?? Where is the
scene??*

He uses this to illustrate that if he had become what those girls were, it would not have been “right” for him. Yet again, this shows how people will be better
off living the journeys meant for them rather than the journeys meant for others.

***Final comments:** This essay received a 4/6, or 1-2-1 (this was curved to an 8/10 … yes, this class was hard).*

***Thesis -** reasonable, defensible (although difficult). Easiest point to get on paper, but hardest to make to match your essay. This is exactly what I did
here.*

***Evidence -** it is specific and relevant, I think that the quote about Harvard girls and his shoes do provide evidence to support description. However, you
did not write about the **importance** of specific words and details, and the line of reasoning doesn’t match the thesis. The commentary does somewhat support
the argument, though, so this is a clear 2.*

***Sophistication -** somewhat clear and persuasive? I think the writing style is not terrible.*

In “Even If I Had a Long Ponytail Back Then,” Haruki Murakami uses **metaphors** to **show how comfortable he is with his own life**. This is first seen in the
essay when Murakami talks about the running shoes he bought. He says that **“they have no gimmicks, no sense of style, no catchy slogan … to the average
consumer, they have little appeal. Yet the soles of these shoes have a solid, reliable feel as you run.”** (Page 3) Here, he uses running shoes as a metaphor
for people’s lives—each person has their own unique fits. Like him, his shoes have no **“gimmicks”** and there is nothing obviously brilliant about them. He
himself says that they have **“no sense of style”** and **“little appeal,”** framing them in an almost disgustingly boring way. This makes it seem like the
essay might be about his regret over being less eye-catching. However, we see that this is not the case, as he also says that the shoes feel **“solid”** and
**“reliable,”** showing that he is comfortable without the **“gimmicks.”** He feels comfortable living his own life. This idea continues throughout the essay,
as he also uses hairstyle as a metaphor. Like the running shoes, it’s something that all runners have, and it’s something that requires preference. Here, he
focuses on the hairstyles of the Harvard girls, saying that **“Most of these girls are small, slim … blond hair in a ponytail … they all look so bright …
attractive … brimming with self confidence … Compared to them, I’m pretty used to losing … but even if I had a long ponytail back then, I doubt if it would have
swung so proudly as these girls’ ponytails do.”** (Page 4) Here, the long ponytail represents everything that the Harvard girls are—bright and brimming with
self confidence, absolutely overflowing with this positive energy. But it is clear that his preference is not the long ponytail, and it represents everything
that he isn’t: **“small,”** **“slim,”** **“bright,”** and **“attractive.”** Additionally, since he says that he is so **“used to losing”** to them, we might
think that this essay is again about his bitterness in his inability to be as *shiny*. However, this is again not the case, as he uses this metaphor to show
that even if he had the long ponytail, it would not have **“swung as proudly as these girls’ do.”** He is not envious of these girls or regretful of his own
life, because he knows he would not have been comfortable in their shoes. Instead, he feels secure in his own, admiring others as they live their own lives.
